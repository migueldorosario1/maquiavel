There is a cruel asymmetry at the heart of contemporary politics. Never has so much rigorous knowledge been produced about power — who holds it, how it is exercised, why it decays. Brazil alone trains, at IESP-UERJ, at UFRJ, at USP and across its federal universities, political scientists of the first rank; the global academy publishes, every year, thousands of peer-reviewed pages on elections, institutions, authoritarianism, representation. And yet the public debate, in Brazil and abroad, has never sounded so much like a shouting match in the dark.

The knowledge exists. The language does not travel.

Maquiavel is born to shorten that distance. We are an international journal of political science — essays, not hard news. We do not chase the hour's headline; we pursue the structures beneath it. When Congress votes, when a court rules, when a democracy trembles anywhere on the planet, our question is not *what happened* but *what explains it* — and who has already studied it seriously.

Our name is a declaration of method. Niccolò Machiavelli founded modern political science precisely by refusing the comforts of wishful thinking: he described politics as it is, not as moralists wanted it to be. Five centuries later, the gesture remains scandalous and necessary. To understand power is not to worship it. It is the first step toward transforming it — and the only antidote to the two diseases of our time: cynicism without analysis, and indignation without method.

Three commitments organize everything we publish.

**Rigor with clarity.** Every essay has grounding — data, literature, sources — and none of it hides behind jargon. If an idea cannot be explained in living prose, it is not yet an idea: it is a footnote looking for an author.

**Three languages, one conversation.** Maquiavel is born trilingual: English, Portuguese and Spanish, with separate pages for each language. Not translations as afterthoughts, but the same conversation held three times, with the respect each language deserves. Political science is written in English; politics is lived in every language. We intend to be the bridge.

**A map, not an island.** Our Archive section keeps a living inventory of political science itself: the journals of Brazil and the world, the thesis repositories, the research centers, the newsletters and the video channels where the discipline actually breathes. A journal that believes in science must begin by showing where science lives.

We are also, unapologetically, a magazine. We believe in the essay as a form — long enough to think, short enough to read, written to survive the week it was published. We believe that a conversation with a political scientist, recorded with care, is itself political science — which is why Maquiavel is also a podcast of debates, each episode becoming an essay. And we believe the scientific vocation must be earned, never proclaimed: our path toward ISSN registration, editorial board and peer review will be announced credential by credential, only as each is actually obtained.

Machiavelli wrote *The Prince* in exile, at night, in a small farm outside Florence — dressing up, he said, to enter the ancient courts of antiquity and converse with the dead. "I am not ashamed to talk with them," he wrote, "and ask them the reason for their actions; and they, in their humanity, answer me."

This journal is our way of joining that conversation — and of extending it, at last, to everyone.

*Welcome to Maquiavel.*
